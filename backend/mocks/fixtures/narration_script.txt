DOCUMENTARY NARRATION — THE HISTORY OF THE INTERNET

In 1969, hidden inside a university lab, four computers were quietly connected for the first time. The world had no idea this modest experiment would one day link all of humanity.

The first message ever sent across this network was supposed to be "LOGIN." The system crashed after just two letters. The internet began with the accidental word "LO."

For years, the network grew in secret — a tool for scientists and the military. But it had a fatal flaw: dozens of separate networks that could not speak to each other.

In 1974, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published a paper that changed everything. TCP/IP — a universal language for machines. Now any network could talk to any other.

Still, the internet was invisible to ordinary people. That changed in 1989, when a quiet physicist at CERN in Geneva wrote a proposal for linking documents across the network.

Tim Berners-Lee called his idea the World Wide Web. His manager wrote "vague but exciting" in the margins and approved it. Two years later, the web went live.

Then came the browser. In 1993, Mosaic brought images to the web and sparked a frenzy. Millions of ordinary people logged on for the first time. The dot-com era had begun.

Money flooded in. Fortunes were made and lost overnight. In 2000, the bubble burst — but from its wreckage rose something far more powerful: Google, Amazon, and eventually, social media.

Today, five billion people are connected. The internet carries the world's commerce, its culture, its science, and its democracies. It has become the infrastructure of everything.

It was built to survive catastrophe. It ended up becoming something those four 1969 computers could never have imagined: the closest thing humanity has ever built to a shared mind.
