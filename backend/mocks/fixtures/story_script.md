HOOK

In 1969, a computer at UCLA sent the first ever message across a network. The word was supposed to be "LOGIN." The system crashed after two letters — leaving only "LO." It was an accidental greeting to the future. A future nobody could have imagined.

ACT 1 - THE ARPANET

The internet was born in a Cold War laboratory. American scientists, funded by the Pentagon, were searching for a communication network that could survive a nuclear attack — one with no single point of failure. Their solution was radical: break information into small packets, send each one independently across a web of connected machines, and let them reassemble at the destination.

On 29 October 1969, four universities were linked. The ARPANET was alive. Within years, researchers discovered something unexpected: what they most wanted to use the network for was not data transfer — it was email. Human connection had found its way into the machine.

ACT 2 - THE LANGUAGE OF THE INTERNET

By the early 1970s, dozens of separate networks existed — but they could not talk to each other. Then, in 1974, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published a paper introducing TCP/IP: a universal protocol that let any network speak to any other. They had invented the internet's grammar. In 1983, every computer on the ARPANET switched to TCP/IP simultaneously. The internet as we know it was born.

Still, it remained invisible to ordinary people — a tool for scientists, researchers, and the military. That changed with one man and one idea conceived in a quiet office in Geneva.

ACT 3 - THE WEB AND THE WORLD

In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee, a physicist at CERN, proposed a system for linking documents across the network using something he called hyperlinks. His boss wrote "vague but exciting" in the margins and approved it. Two years later, the World Wide Web went live. In 1993, the Mosaic browser added images, and the modern internet era began.

What followed was one of the fastest technology adoptions in human history. A billion users by 2005. Two billion by 2010. Five billion by 2020. The dot-com bubble burst and rebuilt itself into something unimaginably larger — social media, cloud computing, smartphones, streaming, and now artificial intelligence — all built on those four humble nodes connected in 1969.

CLOSING

The internet was built to survive catastrophe. Instead, it became the infrastructure of everything — commerce, culture, science, democracy, and daily life. It remains, at its core, what it was on that first night in 1969: a network of people, trying to connect.
